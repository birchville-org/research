## 2. Key Findings & Detailed Analysis

### A. Patterson et al. (Science Advances): Ladakh as a Contact Corridor
::: grammar-box
The sequencing of ten skeletons from Ladakh demonstrates an approximately half-and-half ancestry from Tibetan and North Indian components. Segment length analyses date the onset of admixture to approximately 2,800 BP. This illustrates the permeability of high-mountain routes but does not directly concern the earliest Bronze Age Steppe migration.
:::

### B. Tamil Nadu & the Dravidian Genesis
Madurai Kamaraj University (MKU) announces nine scholarly articles on the burial sites of Konthagai/Keeladi and Adichanallur. These findings are essential for mapping the spread of post-Harappan populations into South India.

### C. Philological Counter-Position on the BMAC Substrate
A contribution by David B. Buyaner (*Indo-Iranian Languages*) problematizes the attribution of certain loanwords (such as terms for "brick") to the BMAC substrate. This is an important methodological discussion, but it does not provide empirical evidence for an Indian homeland model.

Executive Summary
The most important peer-reviewed finding in the current environment is the Science Advances study
by Patterson et al. (July 24, 2026; just outside the strict 30-day window as context): Ten genomes from the Old Lady Spider Cave in Ladakh show a rare, approximately 50:50 split Tibetan–North Indian ancestry for present-day South Asians; admixture began according to segment length analysis approximately 2,800 years ago. This concerns a Himalayan contact zone and is not a direct test of the OIT/AIT question for the Bronze Age.
(Patterson et al., Science Advances)
In August, two important ongoing aDNA projects became public: Madurai Kamaraj
University reported preliminary results of approximately 75,000 markers from human remains from the
sites of Konthagai/Keeladi, Adichanallur, and Sivagalai; the nine announced
scholarly articles had not yet been published. At BHU, samples were taken from an ancient Rajghat
skeleton; radiocarbon dating, aDNA, and isotope analyses are still pending.
(WION report on MKU study; ANI on Rajghat study)
The ongoing transfer of new Rakhigarhi remains to the Anthropological Survey of
India (AnSI) is scientifically significant but does not yet provide new genetic
results: Eight burials were reported in the 2025/26 campaign, and three complete
skeletons and fragments were handed over for examination. (Doordarshan/All India Radio,
Rakhigarhi)
A new overview by Tyagi and Rai explicitly discusses the methodological limits
of aDNA in South Asia, as well as competing interpretations of Rakhigarhi, Swat, and
caste endogamy. A 2026 contribution by David B. Buyaner attacks the BMAC
substrate hypothesis using a specific word ("brick"); this is a philological
counter-contribution, but not new evidence for OIT. (Tyagi & Rai, Proceedings of the Indian National
Science Academy; Buyaner, Indo-Iranian Languages)
The evidence remains asymmetrical: Established research rejects a violent
"invasion" as too crude a model but considers a gradual immigration or
spread of Steppe-related populations after the peak of the Indus Civilization
to be the currently best-supported explanation for the genetic and linguistic historical findings.

OIT arguments for local continuity, Sarasvati, and Vedic chronology remain important
counter-positions, but are not the majority view in international scholarly literature.

New Studies & Finds
1. Ladakh: Himalaya as a Contact Zone, Not an OIT/AIT Decider
Patterson, Mushrif-Tripathy, Devers et al. published on July 24, 2026, in Science Advances
the study “Ancient genomes from Ladakh reveal 2800-year-old admixture between Tibetans
and South Asians” (DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aeb3636). Ten genomes from the Old
Lady Spider Cave at approximately 4,000 m altitude were examined; the individuals date approximately 1,500 years before
present. They were genetically relatively homogeneous and showed approximately equal proportions of a
population well represented by present-day North Indians, and an ancient Tibetan-
related population. From the length of inherited DNA segments, it is concluded that the
admixture began at the latest about 50 generations earlier, i.e., around 2,800 BP.
(Full text/Abstract in PMC)
For the OIT/AIT debate, the finding is indirect: It shows that South Asia's genesis was
shaped by regional contact zones and multiple, temporally offset admixtures. It
proves neither that these people were "Aryans" nor when Indo-Aryan languages entered the
subcontinent. At the same time, the study warns against a monolithic "Steppe to
India" schema: The Himalayan region was an independent corridor between Tibet,
North India, and Inner Asia. The sample of ten individuals from a cave site is
also not generalizable to the Bronze Age Indus region or all of India.

2. Tamil Nadu: 75,000 Markers, But No Published Dataset Yet
In August, WION and the Deccan Herald reported on preliminary work by the Ancient DNA
Lab of Madurai Kamaraj University. From the highly degraded remains of Konthagai
(connected to Keeladi), Adichanallur, and Sivagalai, more than 75,000 genetic markers
were analyzed; smaller marker numbers were reported for Adichanallur and Sivagalai. The
preliminary statement is that the samples showed genetic proximity to present-day South Indian, Sri
Lankan Tamil, and broader South Asian populations. Nine scholarly articles on
ancestry, agriculture, diet, and textile production were announced for later in the year. (WION; Deccan Herald)
Methodologically, a distinction must be made between markers and a published, authenticated genome dataset.
Without raw data, contamination controls, radiometric dating,
coverage values, population models, and peer-reviewed publication, neither
"genetic continuity" in the strong sense nor a statement about the origin of Indo-Aryan
languages can be derived. The report is nevertheless relevant because South India has been
significantly less represented in aDNA research than the Northwest. It could in the future
refine the history of southern populations and their relationships to Indus, Deccan, and Sri Lanka contexts.

3. Rajghat/BHU: New Samples from the Middle Ganges Plain
The Banaras Hindu University launched in August an interdisciplinary investigation of a
skeleton recovered during the Rajghat (Varanasi) excavations in 1957 and stored for decades.
Reports mention a possible age of approximately 1,000 to 2,800 years; the exact
age determination is still pending. Planned investigations include radiocarbon dating, mitochondrial
and – if preserved – Y-chromosomal DNA, stable isotopes, as well as osteological and
paleopathological examinations. One goal is comparison with Harappan/Rakhigarhi
samples to investigate the relationships between the Ganges and Indus regions. (ANI; Indian Express)
The project is a research promise, not a result. Even a successful genome
would initially only document the ancestry of this one person at a specific time.
A connection between genetic continuity, language change, and cultural
transformation would have to be established through many temporally and spatially distributed samples, as well as archaeology
and historical linguistics.

4. Rakhigarhi: New Skeletons and the Expectation of Future aDNA
The ASI reported eight burials in Mound 7 for the 2025/26 excavation season. Three
complete skeletons and other fragments were handed over to the AnSI in Kolkata; planned
investigations include aDNA, stable isotopes, osteology, paleopathology, and
environmental reconstruction. Collaborations with BSIP Lucknow, UCL, BHU, and other
institutions are planned. At the time of reporting, no published genetic results are available
from this new series. (Official report by Doordarshan/All India Radio)
The significance lies in expanding the hitherto very narrow Harappan aDNA base. The
published Rakhigarhi genome from 2019 comes from a single woman; it showed no
detectable Steppe ancestry. This is a strong finding for this person and this
time, but not proof that every Harappan group had the same composition.
Conversely, a later detected Steppe component would not automatically prove a violent
invasion.

5. Mohenjo-Daro: Earlier City Wall Dating
New radiocarbon dates from material at the lower city wall of Mohenjo-Daro date
the earliest reported construction phase to approximately 2700–2600 BCE; later wall phases
extend at least until about 2200 BCE. The findings are associated with Kot-Diji phase pottery and
several construction phases. The Archaeology Magazine summary describes the
new dating as an indication of urban development several centuries older than in
older representations. (Archaeology Magazine, July/August 2026; Arab News on DGAA announcement)
For the debate, this strengthens the chronology of a long, internally differentiated urbanization
in the Indus region; however, it says nothing directly about the language of the city population or about the
origin of later Indo-Aryan speakers. The frequent equation "older Indus city = Veda
in very early times" is therefore an additional interpretative step, not a dating from the
walls themselves.

Linguistic, Philological, and Historiographical Contributions
BMAC Substrate: Concrete Philological Dissent
David B. Buyaner discusses in **“Etymology of some Indo-Iranian cultural terms and the Indo-Iranian substratum theory: I. Indo-Iranian *Hišti- / *Hišta- ‘brick’” (2026, DOI:
10.30842/30346800.2026.2.1.7)** the assumption, advocated by Witzel and others, that part of the
common Indo-Iranian cultural vocabulary derives from contacts with the BMAC culture.
Buyaner argues, using the word for "brick," that a plausible Indo-European
inherited word etymology from 'to dry' must be considered over a BMAC loanword analysis. (Article text)
The contribution illustrates what the linguistic dispute is actually about: not a
single "Aryan" gene, but reconstruction, sound laws, semantic plausibility,
contact zones, and the dating of common innovations. A counter-argument to Witzel's
substrate model is not automatically proof of OIT; the competing etymologies
must be tested against the entire lexical and archaeological corpus.
In the strict July 31–August 30 window, no new study published in Nature, Science, Cell, PNAS, or
a relevant major Indology journal was found that would immediately re-decide Vedic dating or
OIT/AMT. The search for August primarily found follow-up reports, popular classifications, and ongoing projects. As context, the Oxford presentation on the Ṛgveda question remains relevant: The widespread scholarly position places the immigration of Indo-Aryan
speakers in the second millennium BCE and understands it as a gradual process, not a
unified conquest. (Brereton & Jamison, Oxford Academic)

Public and Political Reception
The new Maharashtra textbook edition for Class 6 was controversially discussed in August.
Reports highlight that the depiction of the Vedic diet is formulated as "plant-based"
and an earlier reference to Varna, birth-based caste order, or social
inequality has been removed. This is not an aDNA study, but it shows how scientifically
uncertain or complex questions can be selectively simplified in school narratives.
(Indian Express; India Today)
In parallel, reporting on Rakhigarhi published in June 2026 and circulating further in August
is politically amenable: The phrase "Indus-Sarasvati" is read in parts of the
public debate as evidence of indigenous continuity. Research itself formulates
more cautiously: A missing Steppe finding in a Harappan sample and a later Steppe
component in present-day groups are compatible, without implying a "race" of the
Aryas or a simple identity of Harappans and Vedic composers. The public
polarization between "colonial invasion myth" and "proven indigenous civilization"
inadequately reflects this intermediate position.

State of the Debate
What the AMT/Migration Side Can Currently Explain Well
1. The 2019 Science analysis of 523 ancient individuals finds a temporally and geographically
circumscribed entry of Steppe-related ancestry into South Asia's gene pool after the
decline of the Indus Civilization; in modern populations, this proportion is
variably high and often male-biased. (Narasimhan et al., Science)
2. The 2019 Cell genome from Rakhigarhi shows no detectable Steppe ancestry in
this single Mature Harappan sample, thus supporting the sequence "Harappan
component before later Steppe contribution." (Shinde et al., Cell)
3. Indo-Iranian proximity, reconstructed chariot/horse terminology, BMAC contacts, and the Swat
sequence are interpreted in established historical linguistics and archaeology as
consistent, but not entirely identical, evidence for mobility and language change.
The conclusion is predominantly migration/interaction, not "invasion" in the Wheeler sense.

What OIT and Continuity Models Rightly Emphasize
1. The majority of the present-day Indian population is genetically predominantly derived from much
older South Asian and Iranian-related components; a later Steppe component is
a layer, not a complete population replacement.
2. Archaeological continuity in settlement, craftsmanship, agriculture, and local rituals
contradicts a simple model in which a single wave of conquest replaces the Indus
Civilization.
3. The equation of genes, language, culture, and "people" is inadmissible. Local continuity
can coexist with limited migration and language change.
OIT in the strict sense – India as the origin of the entire Indo-European
language family – remains difficult to reconcile with the current combination of comparative linguistics,
chronology, and aDNA. The critical question is therefore no longer meaningfully
formulated as "invasion or no invasion," but rather: Which groups moved
when, in what magnitude, through which contact zones, and how were languages and
social institutions adopted?

Methodological Assessment
Representativeness: A single Rakhigarhi woman, ten Ladakh individuals, or a
Rajghat skeleton are not "Population India." Statements must be limited by location, time,
sex, social context, and preservation quality.
aDNA and Contamination: Highly degraded DNA requires authenticity and
contamination controls, transparent coverage, mitochondrial/Y-chromosomal
classification, and, if possible, replicated extractions. Preliminary marker counts from
press reports do not replace a published genome.
Admixture is not Language: Genetic influx can correlate with language change,
but does not prove which language the carriers spoke. Conversely, a language

can be spread by small elites, networks, and institutions without a
complete population replacement.
Chronology: Radiocarbon dates date samples or construction phases, not automatically
texts. The dating of Ṛgveda layers must integrate philological relative chronologies,
comparative linguistics, textual transmission, and material contexts.
R1a/L657: Haplogroups are paternal lineages, not ethnicities or "Aryan
genes." Their significance for AIT/AMT depends on temporally dated ancient Y-chromosomes,
phylogenetic branching, and population models; modern frequencies
alone do not decide the debate. The methodological warning against a direct
equation of DNA and historical identities is also central in Thapar's socio-historical
discussion. (Thapar, Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Biology)
Sarasvati/Indus-Sarasvati: A paleochannel, a river history, or a settlement density
can illuminate regional environmental and economic history. It proves neither the
identity of the Rigvedic river nor the language of the groups living there without
independent dating and text/material correlation.
Publication Status: Ongoing projects (MKU, BHU, new Rakhigarhi samples) should remain
open until publication. Press releases, blogs, and politically positioned
essays are valuable for reception history but are not equally valid evidence alongside
peer-reviewed studies.

Open Questions
1. Will the new Rakhigarhi skeletons provide authentic, sufficiently deep genomes,
and will they show homogeneous or regional variation within the Mature Harappan phase?
2. When exactly and through which intermediate populations did Steppe-related ancestry
appear in Swat, Punjab, Haryana, and the Ganges Plain? Are there contemporary male Y-lines that
can refine the frequently asserted R1a attribution?
3. Will MKU and Keeladi samples be published in a way that reveals radiocarbon dates,
contamination controls, coverage, and comparison populations?
4. What will the Rajghat material say about the middle Ganges Plain in the first millennium BCE
or in the early historical period – and how strongly can a single
person be generalized?
5. Can new archaeological contexts better connect the transitions of Late Harappan, Cemetery-H,
Painted Grey Ware, and early Iron Age regions without prematurely
interpreting pottery styles as ethnicities?
6. Which Indo-Iranian loanword candidates actually meet the criteria for
substrate words, and which can be explained by regular inherited word etymologies?
Buyaner's brick analysis is a verifiable individual contribution, not a conclusive solution.
7. When will the Indus script be deciphered in a methodologically robust way? Without a reading, direct
statements about the language of the urban Indus population remain speculative.
8. How will new aDNA findings be presented in textbooks, museum narratives, and political
discourses so that continuity and mobility remain visible simultaneously?

Reference to Malinar Lecture (UZH)
The findings fit particularly well with a critical analysis of the construction of
"Hinduism": Neither "Hindu" nor "Arya" automatically denotes a
biologically homogeneous nation in the Bronze Age. aDNA shows stratifications and contact zones; archaeology
documents practices and settlements; philology reconstructs language stages; later
religious collective terms retrospectively categorize very different traditions. For the
lecture, therefore, the conceptual separation is central: Indus Civilization, Indo-Aryan
language history, Vedic ritual traditions, and modern Hinduism are interconnected
but not identical historical levels. The political charge of the terms
explains why the same data can be narrated as "continuity" or "migration."
