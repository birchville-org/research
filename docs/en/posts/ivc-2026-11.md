## 2. Key Findings & Detailed Analysis

### A. Dholavira, Gujarat, and Early Ceramic Networks
On November 19, 2026, Manisha Kesarwani et al. published in the *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports* combined C, O, and Sr isotope data from tooth enamel of cattle (*Bos*) and water buffalo (*Bubalus*) from Dholavira, which differentiate C3/C4 feeding strategies and regional herd mobility in the Kutch basin. In parallel, Ahana Ghosh (*Ethnoarchaeology*, November 18, 2026) sheds light on everyday mobility routines in Harappan Gujarat, while the Routledge volume *South Asian Ceramics* (November 18, 2026) with contributions by J. M. Kenoyer, K. K. Bhan, K. Krishnan, S. Chakradhari et al., and A. Rossi et al. dates the Indus ceramic presence in Gujarat back to approximately 3700–2900 BCE and secures Ganeshwar ceramics using AMS-14C.

### B. Osteometry, North Pakistan Genomics, and Domestication
Manjulika Gautam et al. (November 9, 2026, Research Square) analyzed 46 adult skeletons from Rupar, Kalibangan, and Lothal (pooled estimated stature 189.3 ± 20.5 cm; no significant site difference, p = 0.21). In *Molecular Biology and Evolution* (November 1, 2026), Zong-Liang Gao et al., using 115 whole genomes from North Pakistan, identify two East Asian migration waves along the southern Himalayan foothills (approximately 4,800 and 1,600 years ago, respectively). Two bioRxiv preprints by Miraszek et al. (November 6, 2026) and Rathod et al. (November 19, 2026) model the Zebu cattle as an admixture of taurine cattle with Indian aurochs and document drought selection in Thar populations, respectively.

### C. Epigraphy: Edition Criticism and Overseas Adaptation
Pallavee Gokhale (*South Asian Studies*, November 6, 2026) criticizes the isolated treatment of the Indus script as an AI mathematical puzzle. Sriharsha Garlapati (November 19, 2026) demonstrates 12.9% sign discrepancies between standard editions and 37% split leakage due to near-duplicates, while *INDUS-DEDUCT* (November 20, 2026) introduces a control benchmark calibrated against hallucinations. At the same time, Erin Rae Toppe (October 26, 2026) shows that 83% of sign pairs on 17 Gulf/Mesopotamian inscriptions are absent in the core area (compared to 49% in control texts, p = 0.0006), indicating adaptations to foreign names in maritime trade.

### D. Paleoclimatology: Late Pleistocene Sutlej Decoupling
Vimal Singh, Sampat Kumar Tandon et al. (*Communications Earth & Environment*, November 7, 2026) demonstrate, using OSL dating and the *Ropar Surface*, that the tectonically controlled displacement of the Paleo-Sutlej occurred between > 60 and 15 ka – millennia before the Harappan florescence. This confirms that settlements on the Ghaggar-Hakra primarily depended on the monsoon. New archives from Kaithal/Haryana (Som Dutt et al., November 3, 2026), the Northeast Himalayas (Yachna Verma et al., October 31, 2026), Toli Taal (Ray & Porinchu, November 17, 2026), and the Indus Delta (Ali et al., November 3, 2026) support the model of gradual agro-ecological adaptation.


## 3. Synthesis & Conclusions

1. **Subsistence and Early Connectivity:** Isotope and ceramic data from Gujarat show a regional anchoring and flexible pastoralism extending deep into the 4th millennium BCE.
2. **Methodological Consolidation in Genetics and Epigraphy:** Rigorous corpus audits displace uncalibrated AI decipherments, while overseas inscriptions demonstrate independent combinatorial patterns.
3. **Clear Separation of Tectonics and Holocene Climate:** The Late Pleistocene Sutlej diversion decouples the Ghaggar-Hakra system from glacial runoff and underscores the role of the 4.2k monsoon weakening.

### 1. Executive Summary

During the reporting period of November 2026, peer-reviewed studies, edited volumes, and preprints refined the chronology, economy, biometry, script methodology, and river dynamics of the Indus Valley Culture (IVC / Harappan Civilization):

1. **Archaeology & Settlement Studies:** A multi-isotope study (δ13C, δ18O, 87Sr/86Sr) on cattle and water buffalo tooth enamel from Dholavira reconstructs pastoralism and herd mobility. The edited volume *South Asian Ceramics* dates the earliest Indus presence in Gujarat to approximately 3700–2900 BCE and verifies Ganeshwar contacts via AMS-14C.
2. **Archaeogenetics & Paleoanthropology:** An osteometry study of 46 skeletons from Rupar, Kalibangan, and Lothal provides long bone estimates without significant site-specific gradients. A genome analysis in *Molecular Biology and Evolution* demonstrates two East Asian migration waves into North Pakistan (approximately 4,800 and 1,600 BP, respectively); a bioRxiv preprint explains the Zebu origin through admixture of taurine cattle with Indian aurochs.
3. **Epigraphy & Script Research:** *South Asian Studies* criticizes the mathematically-cryptographic narrowing of AI script research. A corpus audit reveals 12.9% edition discrepancies and 37% data leakage. Pre-configured tests also show that Indus inscriptions in the Gulf and Mesopotamia form 83% new sign pairs.
4. **Paleoclimatology & De-Urbanization:** OSL datings in *Communications Earth & Environment* prove that the tectonic diversion of the Paleo-Sutlej already occurred between > 60 and 15 ka. Geoarchives from Haryana and the Himalayas support the model of gradual 4.2k monsoon adaptation.

### 2. Archaeology, Urbanism & Settlement Studies

#### 2.1 Dholavira and Gujarat: Animal Husbandry and Mobility
On November 19, 2026, Manisha Kesarwani et al. published in the *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports* an isotope analysis of tooth enamel from cattle (*Bos*) and water buffalo (*Bubalus*) from Dholavira (Khadir Bet, Kutch). Combined δ13C, δ18O, and 87Sr/86Sr data differentiate C3/C4 fodder proportions, water sources, and local versus regional herd movements, clarifying the subsistence basis of this island metropolis. In parallel, Ahana Ghosh (*Ethnoarchaeology*, November 18, 2026) investigates, using ceramic production, craftsmanship, and dietary habits (*taskscapes*), how everyday mobility shaped Harappan Gujarat.

#### 2.2 Ceramic Technology, Ganeshwar, and Ongoing Programs
The volume *South Asian Ceramics* (Kanungo, Rajan & Sivanantham, Eds.), published by Routledge on November 18, 2026, compiles new material science findings:
- **Indus Ceramic Chronology:** Jonathan Mark Kenoyer traces painted decorative systems (6500–3000 BP / 4500–1000 BCE) from early textile and basketry influences through the standardized integration phase (2600–1900 BCE) into the Late Harappan period.
- **Gujarat & Petrography:** Kuldeep Kumar Bhan dates the first migrations into Gujarat to approximately 3700–2900 BCE (Pre-Early Harappan). K. Krishnan demonstrates workshop-specific clay preparations at three sites in Gujarat via thin-section petrography.
- **Ganeshwar & Firing Techniques:** Sudarshan Chakradhari et al. re-evaluate the copper-producing Ganeshwar region (Sikar, Rajasthan) with new AMS-14C dates; Alberto Rossi et al. provide experimental parameters for updraft kilns.

The ERC project AGROCHRONO (Emmanuelle Casanova, 2025–2029) has been documenting the spread of agropastoralism between the Zagros and the Indus Valley (7th–4th millennia BCE) since November 2026. Culturally and politically, the reinstallation of the "Dancing Girl" bronze in the National Museum New Delhi (*Yuge Yugeen Bharat Museum*), the expansion of the Rakhigarhi Museum including a UNESCO application, and references to Indus-Mesopotamian trade at the SCO Civilisational Dialogue Forum (November 20–22, 2026) shaped the reporting.

### 3. Archaeogenetics, Domestication & Paleoanthropology

#### 3.1 Osteometry from Rupar, Kalibangan, and Lothal
While aDNA analyses of the skeletons from Rakhigarhi (Mound 7) are ongoing at AnSI, Manjulika Gautam et al. (November 9, 2026, Research Square) published a long bone study of 46 individuals from Rupar, Kalibangan, and Lothal. The pooled mean estimated stature is 189.3 ± 20.5 cm (with high variability of fragmentary regressions). A geographical trend (Rupar > Kalibangan > Lothal) was not significant (p = 0.21); the femoral robusticity index showed a non-significant trend towards higher values in Lothal (p = 0.093).

#### 3.2 Gene Flow in North Pakistan, Thar, and Zebu Origin
In *Molecular Biology and Evolution* (November 1, 2026), Zong-Liang Gao et al. compared 115 high-coverage genomes of the Burusho (Hunza), Shina, and Hazarewal with 2,029 modern and 4,358 ancient Eurasian genomes. They demonstrate two East Asian migration waves along the southern Himalayan foothills: by Yangtze rice farmers approximately 4,800 years ago (parallel to the Early Harappan phase) and from the Tibetan Plateau approximately 1,600 years ago. No demic evidence is found via the northern *Inner Asian Mountain Corridor*.

Two bioRxiv preprints complement the picture: John L. Miraszek et al. (November 6, 2026) trace Zebu cattle (*Bos indicus*) to an admixture of domesticated taurine cattle with Indian aurochs based on whole genomes (238 selected aurochs and 283 taurine domestication loci). Sachin M. Rathod et al. (November 19, 2026) identified 348 variants in 45 genes (*ZNF385D*, *SGCZ*, *SARDH*, *SLC12A3*) under selection for drought stress in 176 individuals from eight Thar populations.

### 4. Epigraphy, Script Research & Language Hypotheses

#### 4.1 Methodological Corpus Criticism and AI Benchmarks
In *South Asian Studies* (November 6, 2026), Pallavee Gokhale criticizes the fixation on AI puzzles fueled by prize awards (2004 and the 1-million-US-dollar prize from Tamil Nadu) and calls for the integration of material object contexts (*"Wearing Harappan Shoes"*).

Quantitative preprints support this criticism: Sriharsha Garlapati (November 19, 2026) compared 469 objects using the excavation registers of the 1930s between both standard editions. These differ in 12.9% of the signs (95% CI: 11.1–14.9%); random splits across 2,270 texts generate 37% data leakage due to near-duplicates (62% without deduplication), and order tests also respond to four out of seven non-linguistic codes. *INDUS-DEDUCT* (Emre, November 20, 2026) tests over 5,679 inscriptions against Ur-III Sumerian, Linear A/B, and symbol codes without fabricated phonetic readings.

#### 4.2 Indus Signs in the Gulf and Language Substrate
A preregistered analysis by Erin Rae Toppe (October 26, 2026) shows that on 17 Indus inscriptions from the Persian Gulf (Dilmun/Magan) and Mesopotamia, 83% of adjacent sign pairs are absent in the core area – compared to 49% in 334 control texts (p = 0.0006; frequent pairs 67% vs. 23%, p = 0.0002; blind replication on Gulf seals: 86% vs. 49%, p = 0.005). This supports adaptation to foreign names in overseas trade. Madhav Prasad Pokharel (November 5, 2026, *Nepalese Linguistics*) links remnant languages (Kusunda, Nihali, Burushaski) with AASI findings.

### 5. Paleoclimatology, Environmental History & De-Urbanization

#### 5.1 Late Pleistocene Sutlej Displacement
On November 7, 2026, Vimal Singh, Sampat Kumar Tandon et al. in *Communications Earth & Environment* refuted a Late Holocene cessation of Sutlej inflow to the Ghaggar-Hakra system. Using OSL dating, stratigraphy, and the uplifted *Ropar Surface*, they demonstrate that the Paleo-Sutlej tectonically shifted along the *Main Frontal Thrust* already between > 60 and 15 ka. The Ghaggar-Hakra settlements of the Harappan florescence thus primarily depended on the monsoon.

#### 5.2 Monsoon Variability (4.2k Event), Haryana, and Indus Delta
Further geoarchives refine the Holocene desiccation:
- **Haryana Plains (Kaithal):** Som Dutt et al. (*Quaternary Science Reviews*, November 3, 2026) date the change to stabilized floodplains with calcretes and a disturbance around ~8.2 ka in the Ghaggar-Yamuna system (17–7 cal kyr BP).
- **4.2k Event & Garhwal:** Yachna Verma et al. (*Palaeo-3*, October 31, 2026) demonstrate monsoon fluctuations with a pronounced 4.2k event for 4800–3500 yr BP; Meghna Ray and David F. Porinchu (*Quaternary International*, November 17, 2026) reconstruct Holocene humid phases at Toli Taal lake via CT/XRF.
- **Indus Delta:** Muhammad Ali et al. (*Geomorphology*, November 3, 2026) analyze sediment starvation and shoreline dynamics under hydroclimatic pressure.

The findings confirm gradual agricultural and settlement adaptations instead of a sudden river collapse.
