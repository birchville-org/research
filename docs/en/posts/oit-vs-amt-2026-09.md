# Monthly Research Report – Out of India Theory (OIT) vs. Aryan Invasion/Migration Theory (AIT/AMT)

## September 2026

**Research Period:** August 28 – September 27, 2026  
**Research Profile:** academic search (bioRxiv, Nature, Science, Cell, PNAS, Journal of Human Genetics and related indices), web search with monthly filter, and review of original/institutional websites. Older works are only used as methodological and historical context.

## Executive Summary

- Within the strict reporting window, **no new peer-reviewed study was found in Nature, Science, Cell, PNAS, or bioRxiv that directly decides between OIT or AIT/AMT**. The most important recent peer-reviewed reference remains the *Science Advances* study on Ladakh published in July: it dates an approximately half Tibetan- and half South Asian-related admixture to about 2,800 years ago, but assigns neither a specific language nor a direct "Aryan" identity ([Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aeb3636)).
- New and noteworthy is a **Zenodo preprint** on the genetic history of Punjabis (August 28). It synthesizes known findings on Indus, AASI, Iranian-related, and later Steppe-related ancestry and explicitly emphasizes that R1a proves neither ethnicity nor "Aryan" identity; as a preprint, it is not a peer-reviewed primary study ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/22136825)).
- The September report on a lecture by Gyaneshwer Chaubey mentions mitochondrial haplogroups M4b1, M49, and M61 in four teeth from sites in the Syrian Euphrates region. This is an **interesting indication of mobility between South and West Asia**, but due to a lack of published methodology, dataset description, and peer review, it is not yet reliable evidence for a specific migration direction or language spread ([Economic Times/ANI](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/india/indus-valley-and-mesopotamia-uncovering-the-genetic-connection-beyond-trade/articleshow/134110472.cms)).
- For Rakhigarhi, three human skeletons have been forwarded for scientific examination, including DNA tests; **results are not yet available**. The report should therefore be treated as a project announcement, not as a genetic finding ([Panchjanya](https://panchjanya.com/2026/09/14/490983/bharat/secrets-of-a-5000-year-old-civilization-to-be-revealed-three-human-skeletons-sent-for-scientific-analysis/)).
- Archaeologically, the most important new clues are more **regionally historical than migration-decisive**: Barbadgaon in Kutch is so far only provisionally dated to the 11th–13th century CE and not systematically excavated; Kalibangan is reframed historiographically in an opinionated article. Both contribute to the history of landscapes, contacts, and knowledge production, but do not decide between OIT and AMT ([India Today](https://www.indiatoday.in/amp/magazine/up-front/story/20260928-kutch-the-missing-link-2997464-2026-09-18); [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/opinion/kalibangan-harappan-excavation-indus-valley-civilisation/3041986/)).

## New Studies & Finds

### 1. Genetics: Punjabi Preprint and the Limits of Population Genetic Oversimplifications

Zahid Hussain's text *Origin of the Punjabis: A Detailed Genetic Perspective*, published on Zenodo on August 28, is designated as a **preprint** and not a journal article. It is therefore relevant for monthly observation but should be classified significantly below a peer-reviewed primary study in terms of evidence ([Zenodo Metadata](https://zenodo.org/records/22136825); DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.22136825](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22136825)).

The preprint summarizes Punjabi history as a multi-layered model: deep South Asian hunter-gatherer ancestry (AASI as a statistically inferred, not directly sampled component), Iranian-related ancestry in the Indus region, and later incoming Steppe-related ancestry. For the single IVC-associated genome, the lack of detectable Steppe ancestry is emphasized; at the same time, the text itself points out that a single IVC genome cannot represent the entire genetic diversity of a large-scale civilization ([Zenodo Preprint](https://zenodo.org/records/22136825)).

Particularly useful for the debate is the methodological restraint: the preprint explicitly states that "R1a" is a paternal lineage and not an ethnicity; no single Y-chromosome can define the origin of the Punjabis. It also warns against treating ANI and ASI as historical peoples or nations. These statements do not support a simple OIT narrative, but they also correct a popular AIT/AMT variant that directly derives a complete ethnic or cultural identity from a single marker ([Zenodo Preprint](https://zenodo.org/records/22136825)).

### 2. Indus–Mesopotamia: Lecture on Four Teeth from Syria

A report disseminated by ANI on September 12 about a lecture by BHU geneticist Gyaneshwer Chaubey mentions a joint investigation with Polish researchers. Teeth from four individuals from Tell Ashara (ancient Terqa) and Tell Masaikh (ancient Kar-Ashurnasirpal) in the Syrian Middle Euphrates Valley were analyzed; the time span is given as approximately 2650 BCE to 700 CE ([Economic Times/ANI](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/india/indus-valley-and-mesopotamia-uncovering-the-genetic-connection-beyond-trade/articleshow/134110472.cms)).

The report names the South Asian mitochondrial haplogroups M4b1, M49, and M61 and interprets them as an indication that, in addition to goods, people were also mobile along the known Meluhha trade routes. The statement is historically plausible as a research hypothesis, but genetically still narrow: four teeth, mitochondrial rather than comprehensive autosomal data, unclear contamination and dating details, and no publication or DOI mentioned in the report. Thus, the finding is **not a test of the language question** and certainly not a direct test of OIT versus AMT; if the announced analysis is methodologically confirmed, it initially proves contact and mobility ([Economic Times/ANI](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/india/indus-valley-and-mesopotamia-uncovering-the-genetic-connection-beyond-trade/articleshow/134110472.cms)).

### 3. Kashmir/Burzahom: Peer-Reviewed Mitogenome Study as September Context

The Burzahom study, revisited in September, was not newly published but appeared in *Scientific Reports* in 2025: *Ancient mitogenomes from Neolithic, megalithic and medieval burials suggest complex genetic history of Kashmir valley, India*, DOI [10.1038/s41598-025-00568-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-00568-4). The original study examined twelve skeletal samples; complete mitochondrial genomes could be reconstructed from four samples – one Neolithic, one Megalithic, and two Medieval individuals ([Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-00568-4)).

The authors report, among others, M65a in Neolithic Burzahom3 (2009–2002 cal. BCE), U2b2 in Megalithic Burzahom7 (547–580 cal. CE), M30 in Med1, and W4 in Med2. Phylogenetic relationships to Kashmir, Swat, Central Asia, and BMAC are discussed; a possible Central Asian connection is derived from W4, but not a clear migration route ([Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-00568-4)).

The finding is doubly important for the debate. First, it shows that regional continuity and far-reaching connections can coexist. Second, it shows why mitogenome data does not lead to a simple "population replacement" or language assignment: only four complete mitogenomes, particularly few medieval samples, and a connection to Roopkund explicitly described as inconclusive limit the scope of interpretation ([Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-00568-4)).

### 4. Rakhigarhi: Three Skeletons for Examination – Results Pending

A report from September 14 states that the Archaeological Survey of India has sent three human skeletons recovered from Rakhigarhi to the Anthropological Survey of India in Kolkata for detailed examination. Osteological studies and DNA tests for ancestry, relationships, diet, lifestyle, and physical development are planned ([Panchjanya](https://panchjanya.com/2026/09/14/490983/bharat/secrets-of-a-5000-year-old-civilization-to-be-revealed-three-human-skeletons-sent-for-scientific-analysis/)).

The report does not mention the context of the findings, the sex and age of the individuals, laboratory protocol, sequencing, radiocarbon dates, or statistical results. The designation as "Saraswati-Sindhu Civilization" and the claim that Rakhigarhi is the largest city of this civilization due to an area of over 350 hectares are also not supported by a peer-reviewed evaluation there. For OIT/AMT, therefore, only this can be noted: **new sampling and analysis project, no results yet** ([Panchjanya](https://panchjanya.com/2026/09/14/490983/bharat/secrets-of-a-5000-year-old-civilization-to-be-revealed-three-human-skeletons-sent-for-scientific-analysis/)).

### 5. Archaeology: Barbadgaon in Kutch and the Need for Absolute Dating

*India Today* reported on September 18 about Barbadgaon near Sir Creek in western Kutch. Mentioned are about 100 human skeletons, heads oriented westward, terracotta rice processing tools, brick structures, pottery, lamps, and a copper coin. The attribution to the 11th–13th century CE and the Soomra context is explicitly provisional; systematic excavation and absolute dating are still pending ([India Today](https://www.indiatoday.in/amp/magazine/up-front/story/20260928-kutch-the-missing-link-2997464-2026-09-18)).

The significance lies primarily in environmental and entanglement history: rice processing suggests a formerly different water and settlement landscape; nearby Harappan sites show that the region was already settled millennia earlier. However, statements about earlier Indus river corridors or the course of a Garah distributary are marked as research proposals in the article and would need to be verified by paleochannel mapping, sediment analyses, and dating ([India Today](https://www.indiatoday.in/amp/magazine/up-front/story/20260928-kutch-the-missing-link-2997464-2026-09-18)).

### 6. Kalibangan: Historiographical Reassessment Instead of New Primary Excavation

The article *Kalibangan was hiding in plain sight*, published on September 14, is marked as **Opinion**. It reconstructs the research history from Tessitori's investigations to the ASI excavations from 1961 to current demands for re-excavation and museum construction. The chronologies, measurements, and artifact interpretations mentioned there should therefore be read as a journalistic-historiographical presentation and not as new peer-reviewed excavation results ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/opinion/kalibangan-harappan-excavation-indus-valley-civilisation/3041986/)).

For the OIT/AMT debate, the article is nevertheless insightful: it shows how place names, the Ghaggar-Chautang landscape, "Sarasvati" interpretations, and the visibility of Kalibangan are intertwined in national narratives. This intertwining is a subject of the history of science and public knowledge production; it does not replace chronology, language reconstruction, or population genetic analysis ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/opinion/kalibangan-harappan-excavation-indus-valley-civilisation/3041986/)).

### 7. Linguistics/Philology: No Direct September Contribution, but an Adjacent New Article

The academic search yielded no new peer-reviewed contribution in the reporting window that directly re-decides Sanskrit dating, Vedic chronology, or the OIT/AMT question. As a professionally adjacent contribution, just outside the window, Oskar Podlasiński's article on *Ogamadecā*, published on July 31, should be noted. He analyzes a Sanskrit translation from a Middle Persian context based on Avesta citations and examines phonological, morphological, and syntactic deviations from the Pāṇinian system ([Comparative Indo-European Studies](https://journals.akademicka.pl/cis/article/view/7567); DOI: [10.12797/CIS.28.2026.01.12](https://doi.org/10.12797/CIS.28.2026.01.12)).

The article concerns Indo-Iranian textual tradition but, according to the abstract, does not address chronology, OIT, AIT, or AMT. Its relevance is methodological: linguistic forms must be placed in contexts of transmission, translation, and usage; a single Sanskrit word or a later textual form cannot simply be read as direct evidence for a Bronze Age migration ([Comparative Indo-European Studies](https://journals.akademicka.pl/cis/article/view/7567)).

## State of the Debate

### Archaeogenetic Consensus and its Scope

The most robust framework currently remains a **mixture and contact model**, not a model of complete population replacement. The 2019 *Cell* study on Rakhigarhi/I6113 assigned the individual to an Indus-related genetic lineage that combined Iranian-related and deep South Asian components and had no detectable Steppe ancestry; at the same time, the study emphasized that a single individual does not represent the entire Indus population ([Cell](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(19)30967-5)).

The large 2019 *Science* study modeled 523 newly sampled ancient individuals and a total of 837 ancient genomes. It linked Steppe-related ancestry in northern South Asia with later populations, particularly those visible after the mature Indus period, without deriving a simple equation of genetics, language, and "Aryan" identity ([Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aat7487)).

The *Science Advances* study on Old Ladakh shifts the regional focus: it finds an approximately half South Asian- and half Tibetan-related ancestry in seven high-quality individuals; the admixture is dated to about 2,800 years ago. North Indian Brahmins and Dogra serve as statistical proxy sources, not as identity designations for the ancient people. The study does not assign a specific language and does not provide an independent quantitative Steppe estimate for Old Ladakh ([Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aeb3636)).

This evidence is compatible with a **migration or multiple gene flows from the Steppe to South Asia**, but does not necessitate a military "invasion." In serious research, "invasion," "migration," elite mobility, gradual gene flow, and cultural diffusion are analytically distinguished. Conversely, regional genetic continuity does not refute the existence of later migrations. OIT can point to local continuity, language change without major demographic displacement, or reverse diffusion; however, as a comprehensive model of the spread of Indo-Iranian languages from India, it must explain genetics, archaeology, and historical linguistics together.

### R1a/L657, Mitogenomes, and Language

R1a and its sub-branches are uniparental markers. They can reveal historical male lineages and demographic processes but are neither ethnicities nor language families. The current Punjabi preprint explicitly states this limitation and rejects the equation "R1a = Aryan" ([Zenodo Preprint](https://zenodo.org/records/22136825)).

Mitogenomes such as M65, U2b, M30, or W4 primarily capture maternal lineages. The Burzahom study shows that such lineages can persist regionally over long periods and at the same time be connected to Swat, Central Asia, and BMAC; it cannot derive a language history or overall population structure from this alone ([Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-00568-4)).

### Archaeology, Chronology, and "Sarasvati"

Site continuity, river reconstructions, pottery styles, and ritual findings are important data, but each is context-dependent. A site on the Ghaggar, a fire pit, or a horse depiction does not automatically prove a specific linguistic community. Conversely, the absence of an expected artifact can weaken a hypothesis without refuting it alone. Crucial are stratigraphic contexts, absolute datings, comparative series, and the distinction between a find, its interpretation, and later national framing.

## Methodological Assessment

1. **Sample Size and Representativeness:** One Rakhigarhi individual, four Burzahom mitogenomes, or four Syrian teeth do not allow generalization to "the Indus population," "the Aryans," or "the Punjabis." The size and spatial distribution of ancient genomes in South Asia remain central bottlenecks ([Cell](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(19)30967-5); [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-00568-4)).
2. **Proxy Problem:** AASI is not a directly sampled ancient population. qpAdm models use proxy sources like Onge; thus, percentages are model- and proxy-dependent. The current qpAdm guide recommends sensitivity analyses across multiple proxy sets and a formulation like "15% AASI, proxied by Onge," not a seemingly exact historical ethnicity ([Ancestrify](https://www.ancestrify.io/blog/qpadm-models-south-asian-ancestry)).
3. **Genetics is not Language:** A gene flow can be accompanied by language change, but does not have to be. Statistical proximity to modern Brahmins, Dogra, or "Steppe" reference groups is not a naming of the ancient individuals. The Ladakh study explicitly states this limitation ([Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aeb3636)).
4. **Uniparental Markers:** R1a/L657 and mtDNA haplogroups represent single genealogical paths. They are useful for lineage and mobility questions but not sufficient for ethnicity, caste, religion, or linguistic affiliation ([Zenodo Preprint](https://zenodo.org/records/22136825); [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-00568-4)).
5. **Dating:** Provisional periodizations, as with Barbadgaon, must not circulate as absolute chronology. OIT/AMT arguments require synchronized find contexts and robust dating intervals, not just typological or political assignments ([India Today](https://www.indiatoday.in/amp/magazine/up-front/story/20260928-kutch-the-missing-link-2997464-2026-09-18)).
6. **Source Hierarchy:** Peer-reviewed primary studies and supplementary data take precedence over press reports, preprints, opinion pieces, and blogs. In September, for example, the search yielded a hit for "BMAC consensus guidelines," which actually referred to **Bone Marrow Aspirate Concentrate** in regenerative orthopedics and had nothing to do with the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex ([The Wire/PTI](https://m.thewire.in/article/ptiprnews/first-internationally-developed-bmac-consensus-guidelines-unveiled-at-regen-2026-in-mumbai/amp?utm=relatedarticles)). Such hits show why acronyms must be contextually checked.

## Open Questions

- When and in what regional waves did Steppe-related ancestry arrive in South Asia? More directly dated genomes from Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Kashmir, Swat, Gujarat, and the Ganges region are needed.
- How great was the intra-Indus genetic diversity? Further Rakhigarhi samples are needed before statements about "the" Harappan population or an absence of Steppe ancestry can be generalized.
- What is the significance of R1a-Z93/L657 sub-branches in well-dated ancient samples, and how can they be linked to autosomal ancestry and archaeological contexts?
- How strongly do genetic continuity, language change, and cultural adoption overlap? This requires joint models from historical linguistics, archaeology, and population genetics.
- Which local ancestry components are currently obscured by unsuitable proxies? Sensitivity analyses with various AASI and Steppe proxies are indispensable.
- Which Rakhigarhi samples were actually handed over to which laboratory, and when will sequencing data, radiocarbon dates, contamination controls, and supplementary tables be published?
- Can the Syrian mitochondrial findings mentioned in the lecture be verified in a complete publication with raw data, contamination controls, and precise provenance?
- Which find contexts and absolute datings clarify the late and post-Harappan transitions better than the current, often politicized grand narrative?
- How will textbooks be balanced between meaningful decolonization, plural knowledge history, and uncritical civilization glorification?

## Public and Political Reception

NCERT Director Dinesh Prasad Saklani announced in an interview on September 4 that the 2026 textbooks would include a stronger integration of "Indian Knowledge Systems." Mathematics, astronomy, medicine, architecture, music, environmental knowledge, and historical units on Varna, Jati, Rajdharma, and Kautilya were mentioned, among others. The presentation also emphasizes neither rejecting external knowledge nor uncritically glorifying the past; a direct new OIT/AMT textbook dispute is not documented in this interview ([Matrix News](https://web.matrixnews.in/news/N1686964c75ee57e48ba6df3b687e23c7)).

A feature in *The Revealer* in September describes the transnational spread of Hindu nationalist networks and narratives. It covers diaspora, social media, victimhood and demographic narratives, and campaigns to reshape historical perceptions; it is a journalistic analysis and not a new study on OIT/AMT ([The Revealer](https://therevealer.org/hindu-nationalisms-new-frontiers-from-sri-lanka-to-the-united-states/)). For the public debate, this point is nevertheless relevant: genetic and archaeological findings are often incorporated into larger civilization and identity narratives. Scientifically, therefore, it must always be separated what a dataset shows, what an author concludes from it, and what political identity work follows.

## Reference to the Malinar Lecture (UZH)

UZH lists Angelika Malinar as an emeritus professor of Indology; her official research fields include Hinduism in history and the present, Sanskrit literature, Indian philosophy, and exchange relations between India and Europe in modernity ([University of Zurich – Profile Angelika Malinar](https://www.aoi.uzh.ch/en/indologie/personen/emeriti/malinar.html)). In the sense of a critical-historical lecture perspective, the OIT/AMT debate is therefore not just a question of "which people lived where first," but also a case study on the construction of categories.

Three observations are particularly relevant for the lecture: First, "Hinduism," "Arya," "Indian," "Dravidian," "Brahmin," and "Sarasvati-Sindhu" are not timeless, self-explanatory units, but must be contextualized textually, institutionally, and reception-historically. Second, modern civilization narratives arise from the connection of philology, archaeology, colonial knowledge, nationalism, and contemporary politics. Third, an objective presentation requires thinking about continuity and change simultaneously: genetic continuity can coexist with migration, language change, and religious new formations. Precisely this analytical separation corresponds to a critical construction of the subject "Hinduism," instead of presupposing it as an unchanging biological or cultural essence ([University of Zurich – Profile Angelika Malinar](https://www.aoi.uzh.ch/en/indologie/personen/emeriti/malinar.html)).
