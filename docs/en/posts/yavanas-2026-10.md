# Yavanas with Kharoṣṭhī Pen: Inaugural Report & Baseline

::: note-box [Executive Summary]
This inaugural report launches the third research series of the Birchville Blog. It formulates the research question, systematizes the empirical baseline, and defines the methodological framework: What role did Greek-speaking administrative elites (*Yavanas*) and their chancery practices (ca. 2nd century BCE – 2nd century CE) play in the first written fixation of Buddhist texts in the regional Prakrit language Gāndhārī in the Taxila–Swat region? Evidence from Kharoṣṭhī epigraphy, birch bark manuscripts, and urban archaeology points to a dynamic interplay of older Aramaic scribal traditions with Hellenistic administrative structures.

## 1. Disciplinary Evidence Matrix

In the greater Gandhāra region (Taxila, Peshawar Basin, Swat Valley), three distinct writing and chancery cultures overlapped between 300 BCE and 200 CE: the Imperial Aramaic tradition of the Achaemenids and Mauryas, the Greek administrative language of the Indo-Greek kings, and local Gāndhārī literacy in Kharoṣṭhī.

| Evidence / Monument | Dating | Language / Script | Scientific Finding & Relevance | Confidence |
|:--------------------|:-------|:------------------|:-------------------------------|:-----------|
| **Aramaic Inscription of Taxila (Sirkap, KAI 273)** | ca. 260 BCE (Aśoka attribution) | Imperial Aramaic | Attests Aramaic as official chancery language in Taxila before the beginning of Greek rule. Mentions *Maran Priyadars* ("our Lord Priyadarśi") twice. Origin milieu of the Kharoṣṭhī script. | Very High (In situ marble stele) |
| **Relic Stupa of Meridarch Theodoros/Theodotos (Swat, CKI 32)** | 1st century BCE | Gāndhārī / Kharoṣṭhī | Greek administrative title *meridarkh* (*meridarches*, district governor) in an Indian language. A provincial official donates relics of Buddha Śākyamuni "for the welfare of the many" (*bahujana-hitaye*). | Very High (Original object Lahore Museum) |
| **Meridarch Copper Plate (Shāhpur, Taxila)** | Indo-Greek period (ca. 1st century BCE) | Gāndhārī / Kharoṣṭhī | Second evidence of a Meridarch, this time directly in Taxila: Documents a stupa erection with wife in honor of parents. Administrative elites appear as primary donors. | High (Epigraphic primary finding) |
| **Macedonian Month Names in Inscriptions (Salomon)** | Primarily late Saka and Kushan period | Gāndhārī / Kharoṣṭhī | Eight Macedonian calendar months (e.g., *Panemos*, *Gorpiaios*, *Apellaios*, *Xandikos*) are attested in Buddhist Kharoṣṭhī donation inscriptions. | **Rabatak Inscription of Kaniṣka I.** | ca. 127 CE | Bactrian in Greek cursive script | Documents the conscious imperial shift from Greek chancery language to Bactrian ("Aryan language"). Forms the chronological endpoint of Greek administrative influence. | Very High (Monumental stele in situ) |

## 2. The Central Research Question and its Three Dimensions

The research series investigates the core question:

::: grammar-box
**What role did Greek-speaking administrative elites and their scribal practices (ca. 2nd century BCE – 2nd century CE) play in the first written fixation of Buddhist texts in Gāndhārī in the Taxila–Swat region – and can this transition from administrative to manuscript culture be proven archaeologically, epigraphically, and linguistically?**
:::

From this, three interdisciplinary sub-questions arise:

1.  **Language and Script Contact:**
    What formal traces of Greek administrative practice (title structures, calendar systems, regnal year formulas) are found in Kharoṣṭhī inscriptions – and in what relation do these stand to the older Aramaic scribal tradition from which Kharoṣṭhī once emerged?
2.  **Gāndhārī Philology and Palaeography:**
    Do the oldest birch bark manuscripts (British Library Collection, Schøyen, Senior Collection, Islamabad Collection) show specific characteristics in formulaic structure, ductus, abbreviations, and writing routines that point more to trained secular administrative scribes than to a purely monastic manuscript tradition?
3.  **Urban Topography and Archaeology (Taxila/Sirkap & Swat):**
    Where in the urban space can administrative centers, chanceries, donor inscriptions, and monastic libraries be located? How do sacred buildings, palace areas, and monumental structures like Jandial D on the urban arterial roads relate to each other?

## 3. Four Working Hypotheses in Systematic Comparison

For methodological testing, four competing explanatory models are formulated:

| Hypothesis | Core Model Statement | Pro-Arguments | Contra-Arguments |
|:-----------|:---------------------|:--------------|:-----------------|
| **H1: Chancery Continuity (Aramaic-Mauryan)** | Gāndhārī literacy grew directly out of the Imperial Aramaic scribal organization of the Achaemenid and Mauryan periods. Greek rule formed only a superficial superstructure. | Kharoṣṭhī is palaeographically undoubtedly derived from the Aramaic script. Aramaic monumental steles in Taxila (Sirkap) and Laghman attest to established chanceries before the Indo-Greeks. | Widespread mass transmission of Buddhist texts only begins from the 1st century BCE. Greek loan titles (*meridarkh*) and Hellenistic calendars dominate precisely this era. |
| **H2: Administrative Training (Indo-Greek)** | The Indo-Greek and early Indo-Scythian chancery specifically trained bilingual scribes for coinage, tax lists, and land deeds. This professional infrastructure was adopted by Buddhist monasteries. | Bilingual coin minting (Greek / Kharoṣṭhī); Greek administrative titles in local syntax; adoption of precise dating formulas in sacred inscriptions. | So far, no secular Indo-Greek administrative documents on papyrus or leather have been preserved in Taxila. Month names appear frequently only in the later Kushan period. |
| **H3: Patronage & Elite Donations** | Hellenized elites functioned as wealthy donors in the relic cult. The written fixation of Buddhist texts followed the representational practice of this aristocracy and not the administrative apparatus. | Explicit Meridarch donations in Swat (Theodotos) and Taxila. Relic vessels represent the oldest reliably datable Buddhist written carriers in the region. | The vast majority of epigraphically attested donors bear Indian, Scythian, or Iranian names. Patronage explains donatives, but not the emergence of entire canon libraries. |
| **H4: Urban Densification Area (Sirkap Topography)** | In the Hippodamian-planned city layouts of Sirkap, administration, workshops, and monastic complexes were spatially so close that an automatic transfer of writing techniques occurred. | Spatial proximity of stupas, palace complex, and monumental buildings (Jandial D) along the northern axis of Sirkap. Discovery of the Shāhpur plate in the vicinity of Taxila. | Function of Jandial D still incompletely understood (temple, palace, or outpost?). Primary contexts of many manuscripts unclear, as often acquired through the art market. |

---

## 4. Chronological Desiderata & Research Outlook

The central historical research problem lies in a significant temporal asymmetry:
- The political rule of the Indo-Greeks in Gandhāra ended in the course of the 1st century BCE (replacement by Indo-Scythians / Indo-Parthians).
- However, the overwhelming number of preserved Gāndhārī birch bark manuscripts and the dense series of Macedonian month datings date to the 1st and 2nd centuries CE (under the Kushan rulers from Kujula Kadphises to Kaniṣka I.).

Future monthly reports in this series will therefore analyze how much of this chancery practice was genuinely "Hellenistic," to what extent Sakas and Kushans adapted Greek administrative routines, and what new impulses emerge from ongoing long-term projects (Munich Academy project *Buddhist Manuscripts from Gandhāra*, Sydney *Catalog of Gāndhārī Texts*, ISMEO excavations in Swat).

### 1. Introduction: A Greek Official Donates Buddha Relics

In the 1st century BCE, a man named Theodotos (in older readings Theodoros) dedicated a relic complex in the Swat Valley in honor of the exalted Buddha Śākyamuni. The inscription on the tiny steatite stupa (Catalog of Gāndhārī Texts, CKI 32; now in the Lahore Museum) reads in the Prakrit language Gāndhārī and in the Kharoṣṭhī script:

> *Theudorena meridarkhena pratithavida ime sarira Sakamunisa bhagavato bahujana-hitaye.*  
> ("By the Meridarch Theodotos these relics of the exalted Śākyamuni were dedicated for the welfare of the many.")

This singular epigraphic finding condenses the fascinating encounter of three worlds in a single archaeological artifact:
1.  **The Title:** *Meridarkh* derives directly from the Greek administrative apparatus (*meridarches*, official of a regional subdivision / Meris, known from Ptolemaic and Seleucid chancery systems).
2.  **The Language and Script:** The Prakrit of Northwest India (Gāndhārī), written in the right-to-left Kharoṣṭhī – a script developed two centuries earlier under Achaemenid administration from the Imperial Aramaic chancery script.
3.  **The Religious Context:** The veneration of the physical relics of the Buddha for the supra-individual salvation of the community (*bahujanahitāya*).

A short time later, a second *Meridarch* appears on an engraved copper plate in Shāhpur near Taxila. This unequivocally proves: Greek or Hellenized administrative elites (*Yavanas* in Indian parlance, from Old Persian *Yauna* / Ionians) were not merely military occupiers, but deeply integrated into local society, its administration, and religious donation networks.

### 2. The Overlap of Three Writing and Chancery Cultures

The greater Taxila–Swat region functioned for centuries as one of Asia's most important hubs. Here, three distinct writing systems met and overlapped:

#### 2.1 The Imperial Aramaic Precursor (Achaemenids and Mauryas)
When the Achaemenid Empire under Darius I established the satrapies of Gandāra and Hiduš, it instituted Imperial Aramaic as the official administrative language. This practice outlasted the fall of the Persian Empire. The Mauryan presence under Emperor Aśoka left a monumental marble stele in pure Aramaic (KAI 273) around 260 BCE in Sirkap (Taxila), addressing Aśoka as "our Lord Priyadarśi." From the cursive script of these Aramaic scribes, Kharoṣṭhī developed locally, adapted to the phonology of the Indo-Aryan dialect Gāndhārī.

#### 2.2 The Hellenistic Chancery (Indo-Greeks)
With the expansion of the Graeco-Bactrian rulers (Demetrius, Menander I Soter) into Gandhāra around 180 BCE, the Greek language gained entry. Greek was the language of the court, the military, and finance. Indo-Greek coins established a strict bilingualism model: The obverse bore the Greek royal titulature in the Greek alphabet (*Basileos Soteros Menandrou*), the reverse the exact translation in Gāndhārī in Kharoṣṭhī script (*Maharajasa tratarasa Menadrasa*). This necessarily required an apparatus of scribes and mint masters trained in both systems.

#### 2.3 The Written Fixation of the Buddhist Dharma
Parallel to or immediately following this bilingual chancery practice, one of the most momentous transformations in Asian intellectual history occurred: The Buddhist doctrinal tradition, previously memorized purely orally for centuries, was fixed in writing on birch bark scrolls. The earliest surviving manuscripts (Islamabad Collection, Senior Scrolls, British Library Fragments) are radiometrically dated from the 1st century BCE onwards.

### 3. Detailed Analysis of the Four Working Hypotheses

#### 3.1 H1: Chancery Continuity (Aramaic-Mauryan Bearers)
This thesis postulates that the scribal class of Gandhāra continuously existed from Achaemenid times through the Mauryas to the Kushan era. Kharoṣṭhī is structurally an Aramaic script with diacritical vowel marks. According to Rachel Mairs, supra-regional chancery schools existed that preserved formulaic traditions.
*Critical Objection:* Why did the written fixation of monastic literature not begin already under the Mauryas, who possessed a gigantic bureaucratic apparatus? Why do we find Greek official titles and datings in Buddhist texts, but no Mauryan titulatures?

#### 3.2 H2: Administrative Training as Infrastructure Provider
According to this model, the Hellenistic state created a demand for scribes who used Kharoṣṭhī for secular documents, tax collection, and decrees. Monasteries recruited their scribes from the same scriptoria or had texts produced by professional chancery scribes.
*Critical Objection:* Direct profane administrative documents on organic material from the Indo-Greek period are missing in the humid alluvial soil of Taxila. Secular Kharoṣṭhī documents are only attested from later centuries (Niya, Xinjiang).

#### 3.3 H3: Patronage and Elite Donations
Literacy primarily arose in the context of the donation system. Donors had their donations immortalized on relic containers, slate plates, and copper tablets. The monumentalization of the donation stimulated the need to also record the associated doctrinal texts (Sūtras, Avadānas) as material gifts for stupas.
*Critical Objection:* Donor inscriptions are formally standardized votive formulas; they do not explain the philological mastery required for the composition of complex scholastic treatises (Abhidharma) on birch bark.

#### 3.4 H4: Urban Space Taxila / Sirkap
The excavations of Sirkap show a remarkable architectural densification: A Greek-influenced grid city with rectangular street axes, where residential houses, workshops, stupas, and palace buildings existed immediately next to each other. The monumental structure Jandial D north of the city wall illustrates the fusion of classical Ionic architecture with local ritual functions.
*Critical Objection:* John Marshall's stratigraphic records from the 1910s to 1930s are chronologically imprecise by modern standards. Many finds have been re-dated retrospectively. Furthermore, most manuscript finds do not come from regulated excavations in Sirkap, but from cave caches and stupas in Bajaur, Hadda, or Swat.

---

### 4. The Chronological Paradox: Sakas and Kushans as Heirs of the Yavanas

A fundamental methodological desideratum is the clarification of chronology. When Gāndhārī manuscript production reached its first peak in the 1st and 2nd centuries CE, the kingdoms of the Indo-Greeks had long since fallen. Rulers of the Indo-Scythians (Azes I and II) and the Great Kushans ruled the land.

How is the survival of Hellenistic chancery elements explained?
-   **Macedonian Calendar:** Richard Salomon has shown that months like *Xandikos*, *Artemisios*, or *Apellaios* continued to be used in Kharoṣṭhī inscriptions of the Kushan kings. The Hellenistic solar calendar was simply more practical for trade and imperial administration than the purely lunar months of the Indian interior.
-   **Chancery Tradition:** Institutions survive dynastic changes. Saka and Kushan rulers adopted established scriptoria and their formulas, as the Rabatak inscription proves: Even under Kaniṣka I (ca. 127 CE), the imperial administration was initially conducted in Greek, before Kaniṣka, by decree, elevated the Bactrian language in Greek script to the imperial language.

### 5. Synthesis and Research Outlook

The series "Yavanas with Kharoṣṭhī Pen" will accompany the current progress of knowledge at the interface of Gāndhārī philology, epigraphy, and archaeology in the coming monthly reports:
1.  **Palaeographic Analyses:** Progress of the working group at the Bavarian Academy of Sciences and Humanities (LMU Munich) on the digitization and script characterization of Kharoṣṭhī ductus (project duration until 2032).
2.  **New Finds in the Swat Valley:** Archaeological campaigns by ISMEO and the Department of Archaeology Khyber Pakhtunkhwa on new inscriptions and relic deposits.
3.  **Lexicography of Gāndhārī:** Systematic evaluation of the *Dictionary of Gāndhārī* for Greek loanwords in administrative, military, and ritual vocabulary.
