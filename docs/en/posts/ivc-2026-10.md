## 2. Key Findings & Detailed Analysis

### A. Mohenjo-Daro: Redating of the Kot Diji Fortification
One of the most remarkable re-evaluations concerns the chronology of Mohenjo-Daro (Sindh, Pakistan). Under the direction of the *Directorate General of Antiquities and Archaeology Sindh* in cooperation with international specialists (including Jonathan Mark Kenoyer), systematic re-excavations and five new high-precision AMS-14C dates were carried out at the western edge of the Stupa Mound.

**Correction of a 70-year-old research error:** The approximately 7-meter-thick mudbrick structure uncovered by Sir Mortimer Wheeler in 1950 was then interpreted as a flood protection embankment. The new stratigraphic and architectural analyses now unequivocally prove a monumental city fortification wall of the pre-classical Kot Diji phase (ca. 3300–2600 BCE). ### B. Regional Craft Centers and Maritime Trade Routes: Lothal and Kutch
In Gujarat, the ASI has intensified excavations in the ancient port city of Lothal (in preparation for the *National Maritime Heritage Complex*). The findings confirm that Lothal remained an active transshipment center for semi-precious stones (carnelian, agate), faience, and marine shells even during the transitional phase to post-urbanism (after 1900 BCE). Contemporary excavations in Lakhapar (Kutch) show that fortified outposts had already opened up the arid coastal hinterland for maritime raw material transfer around 3300 BCE.

### C. Rakhigarhi Mound 7: Laboratory Analyses of the aDNA and Isotope Pipeline
Following the conclusion of the 2025/26 excavation campaign, the ASI transferred three complete skeletons and other osteological remains from the necropolis of Mound 7 in Rakhigarhi to the AnSI in Kolkata and the BSIP in Lucknow. Ongoing work includes:
- **Ultrashort-fragment method:** Optimized for highly degraded tropical DNA from fluctuating groundwater zones.
- **Strontium (87Sr/86Sr) and oxygen isotope analyses:** Examination of tooth enamel to distinguish local inhabitants from long-distance migrants from the Balochistan highlands or the coast.
- **qpAdm modeling:** Confirmation of the two-stream model (autochthonous AASI hunter-gatherers + basal Iran Neolithic lineage without steppe or Anatolian admixture).

### D. Epigraphy: NLP Models Against the Non-Script Thesis
Prompted by the 1 million USD *Iravatham Mahadevan Prize*, research teams are increasingly employing NLP and statistical entropy models. Conditional entropy analyses refute the thesis of Farmer & Witzel (pure pictograms): the Indus inscriptions show significant syntactic regularities of logosyllabic writing systems. The current focus is on rebus matching against reconstructed Proto-Dravidian word roots, with linguists insisting on strict criteria to avoid spurious correlations.

### E. Paleoclimate: Megadroughts and Orderly De-Urbanization
A quantitative study in *Communications Earth & Environment* reconstructed monsoon dynamics between 4400 and 3400 BP using speleothems from the Dharamjali Cave. In addition to several droughts lasting over 85 years, an uninterrupted megadrought of 113 years duration affected the basin between approximately 1580 and 1470 BCE (aridification of 65 to 91% of the settlement area). Pollen profiles from the BSIP (Deoria Tal) document the drying up of perennial rivers in the Ghaggar-Hakra system. Archaeobotanical finds (including Jognakhera) demonstrate successful adaptation: a shift from winter cereals to summer monsoon-fed millets and an orderly migration to the wetter plains of the Yamuna and Ganges.


## 3. Synthesis & Conclusions

The findings of the reporting month October 2026 paint a new, differentiated picture of the Indus Culture:
1. **Extended Urbanization Chronology:** Monumental architecture and fortification walls already begin in the early 4th millennium BCE (Kot Diji).
2. **Genetic and Demographic Continuity:** The autochthonous AASI/Iran Neolithic profile remains the empirical reference for the IVC population.
3. **Resilient Transformation:** Instead of an apocalyptic collapse, the civilization responded to centuries of extreme aridification with flexible small-scale agricultural adaptation and eastward migration.

### 1. Executive Summary

During the reporting period September/October 2026, new multidisciplinary research on the Indus Valley Culture (IVC / Harappan Civilization) yielded crucial advancements in four key areas:

1. **Archaeology & Urbanism:** The evaluation of new excavation campaigns and radiocarbon dates in Mohenjo-Daro (Sindh, Pakistan) confirmed that the massive, approximately 7-meter-thick mudbrick perimeter wall west of the Stupa Mound – interpreted by Mortimer Wheeler in 1950 as a flood protection embankment – represents a fortified city wall of the Kot Diji phase (ca. 3300–2600 BCE). This shifts the beginning of urban monumental architecture at the site significantly further back into the 4th millennium BCE. In parallel, renewed excavations by the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) in the port city of Lothal and at Early Harappan sites such as Lakhapar (Kutch) expanded the understanding of regional trade and craft continuities.
2. **Archaeogenetics & Paleoanthropology:** Under the coordination of the Anthropological Survey of India (AnSI) and the Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences (BSIP), skeletons recovered from the necropolis of Rakhigarhi (Mound 7) in summer 2026 were moved into the laboratory analysis phase. The ongoing aDNA and isotope analyses are intended to significantly expand the statistical robustness of the core genetic profile of the Harappan population (two-stream model: autochthonous South Asian hunter-gatherers [AASI] + pre-agricultural Zagros-/Iran-related pastoralist and farmer component) and clarify mobility patterns through strontium and oxygen isotopes.
3. **Epigraphy & Script Research:** Prompted by the Iravatham Mahadevan Prize, awarded in 2025, the use of machine learning, NLP, and discriminative statistical models for deciphering the Indus script has intensified. New computational syntheses and sign-alignment models systematically test the hypothesis of a Proto-Dravidian language basis against non-linguistic symbol systems, with the academic community insisting on strict philological validation standards.
4. **Paleoclimatology & De-Urbanization:** High-resolution paleoclimate profiles and speleothem data (including from Dharamjali Cave) as well as BSIP sediment cores provide evidence of a series of intense, decades-long dry periods between 4400 and 3400 BP. In particular, a 113-year drought cycle in the 2nd millennium BCE precisely coincides with the transformation and settlement shift from the central and western Indus Basin eastward into the Ganges succession.

### 2. Archaeology, Urbanism & Settlement Studies

#### 2.1 Mohenjo-Daro: Redating of the Kot Diji Fortification
One of the most remarkable archaeological re-evaluations concerns the chronology of Mohenjo-Daro. Under the direction of the Directorate General of Antiquities and Archaeology Sindh in cooperation with international specialists (including Jonathan Mark Kenoyer), systematic re-excavations and five new high-precision AMS-14C dates were carried out at the western edge of the Stupa Mound.

The excavation results revise a misunderstanding over 70 years old: the approximately 7-meter-thick mudbrick structure uncovered by Sir Mortimer Wheeler in 1950 was then classified as a mere river embankment against monsoon floods. The new stratigraphic and architectural analyses now prove that it is a monumental city fortification wall, already dated to the pre-classical Kot Diji phase (ca. 3300–2600 BCE). Mohenjo-Daro thus did not emerge suddenly as a planned urban blueprint project of the mature Harappan phase (from 2600 BCE), but has at least seven centuries of longer, continuous settlement and fortification history.

#### 2.2 Regional Craft Centers and Maritime Trade Routes: Lothal and Kutch
In Gujarat, the ASI has intensified excavations in the ancient port city of Lothal to re-document the port structures, tidal canalization, and bead manufacturing facilities in preparation for the establishment of the National Maritime Heritage Complex (NMHC). The new findings confirm that Lothal continued to function as a supra-regional transshipment center for semi-precious stones (carnelian, agate), faience, and marine shells even during the transitional phase to post-urbanism (after 1900 BCE). At the same time, finds from Lakhapar in the Kutch district demonstrate that fortified outposts had already opened up the arid coastal hinterland for maritime raw material transport around 3300 BCE.

### 3. Archaeogenetics & Paleoanthropology

#### 3.1 The Rakhigarhi Mound 7 Project: Expansion of the Genomic Sample
Archaeogenetic research into the Indus Civilization has so far primarily relied on a single skeleton directly sequenced from Rakhigarhi (individual I6113) and eleven genetic migrants ("Indus Periphery Cline") from Shahr-i-Sokhta (Iran) and Gonur Depe (BMAC, Turkmenistan). This sample showed a distinct profile: a basal West Eurasian component that diverged from Iranian pastoralist lineages before the development of agriculture, combined with autochthonous South Asian hunter-gatherer ancestry (AASI), completely free of Pontic-Caspian Steppe ancestry or Anatolian early farmer gene flow.

To confirm the representativeness of this finding, the ASI, after the conclusion of the 2025/2026 excavation campaign, transferred three complete skeletons and other osteological remains from the necropolis of Mound 7 in Rakhigarhi to the central anthropological institute AnSI in Kolkata. In collaboration with the Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences (BSIP) and international working groups, the following are currently underway:
- **Optimized aDNA extraction:** Use of ultrashort-fragment methods to counter the extreme thermal DNA degradation conditions of the Indian alluvium.
- **Strontium and oxygen isotope analyses:** Examination of tooth enamel to differentiate local inhabitants from long-distance migrants from the Balochistan mountains or the coast.
- **Paleopathological profiling:** Recording of nutritional deficiencies and infection markers in late urbanism.

#### 3.2 Methodological Discussion: qpAdm Modeling and the AASI Reference
Current methodological work delves into the stability of qpAdm rotation models for South Asian populations. Since modern proxies (such as the isolated Onge of the Andaman Islands) must be used for the AASI component due to the lack of fossil DNA from undisturbed Pleistocene finds, population geneticists are evaluating proximal models in which the Indus Periphery individuals serve as the primary reference for the indigenous Indian population. The analyses underscore the demographic continuity of Harappan genetics in the modern South Asian population across all language families.

### 4. Epigraphy, Script Research & Language Hypotheses

#### 4.1 Computational Models and the Iravatham Mahadevan Prize
Since the announcement of the one-million-dollar Iravatham Mahadevan Prize in early 2025, computational epigraphy of the Indus script has seen an unprecedented increase in modeling approaches:
- **Structural Text Analysis via NLP:** Researchers use sequence models and n-gram statistics to quantify the rigid positional constraints of signs on seals, copper plates, and clay tablets. Since the average inscription length is only 4.6 characters, the focus of modern AI systems is on demonstrating substitution patterns and grammatical markers.
- **Synthetic Baselines Against Random Models:** Statistical discrimination analyses (including via conditional entropy) once again refute the thesis of Steve Farmer and Michael Witzel that the Indus signs are purely non-linguistic pictograms. The system shows significant syntactic regularities typical of written language in logoconsonantal or logosyllabic systems.

#### 4.2 Rebus Principle and Proto-Dravidian vs. Indo-Aryan
In linguistic classification, two directions continue to dominate:
1. **The Dravidian Hypothesis (Parpola Tradition):** Recent automated sign comparisons attempt to systematically map homophonous meanings (rebus principle) to reconstructed Proto-Dravidian word roots (e.g., fish sign *mīn* = star/fish). This approach is supported by structural parallels to South Indian megalithic graffiti.
2. **Critique and Validation:** Linguists caution against methodological pitfalls: computer-generated matches without bilingual control texts (missing Rosetta Stone) risk cognitive spurious correlations. A formal decoding criterion requires consistent grammar and intercultural plausibility of proper names in Mesopotamian long-distance trade (Meluḫḫa).

### 5. Paleoclimatology, Environmental History & De-Urbanization

#### 5.1 Centuries-Long River Droughts and the 4.2k Climate Event
A groundbreaking study from Gandhinagar in *Communications Earth & Environment* provided new quantitative data on the hydroclimatic stress on the Indus Civilization. Using high-resolution speleothems from Dharamjali Cave and lake sediments, researchers reconstructed monsoon activity between 4400 and 3400 BP:
- Several prolonged river droughts occurred, each lasting over 85 years.
- Between approximately 3531 and 3418 BP (ca. 1580–1470 BCE), an uninterrupted megadrought of 113 years duration affected the basin, resulting in an aridification of 65 to 91 percent of the settlement area.

#### 5.2 Regional Transformation Instead of Catastrophic Collapse
In parallel, pollen analyses by the BSIP from sediment cores of Deoria Tal show a drastic decline in moisture-loving tree species (shift in oak-pine ratio) around 4250 cal yr BP, documenting the drying up of perennial rivers in the Ghaggar-Hakra system.

Archaeobotanical findings (including from Jognakhera and Haryana) paint a picture of a highly developed agricultural adaptation strategy: instead of a sudden military or disease-induced collapse, the Harappan population responded with a gradual de-urbanization, a shift from large-scale winter cereal cultivation (wheat, barley) to diversified summer monsoon-fed millets, and an orderly migration eastward into the wetter plains of the Yamuna and Ganges.
