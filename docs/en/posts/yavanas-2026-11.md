## 2. Key Findings & Detailed Analysis

### A. Bajaur Fragment BC9r: Statecraft and Scholasticism on a Single Support
On November 4, 2026, Andrea Schlosser published a study in the *Indo-Iranian Journal* on fragment 9 of the Bajaur collection (`BC9r`, palaeographically 1st–2nd century CE). While the verso transmits a Buddhist scholastic treatise in Gāndhārī, the recto contains 39 Āryā stanzas on kingship (*rājanīti*), attributed to a Cakṣuka, covering topics such as state building, councils, treasury, and foreign policy in highly Sanskritized Gāndhārī. This secondary or dual use directly links secular governmental culture with the same Kharoṣṭhī birch bark practice.

### B. From ADAB and Menander I to Kaniṣka and the Bactrian Eras
Several November publications bridge the gap between epochs: Marco Ferrario (November 15, 2026) analyzes the Achaemenid chancery basis through the Aramaic Documents from Ancient Bactria (ADAB), while Kadwal & Khakalay (November 14, 2026) examine their script-typological transition to Kharoṣṭhī. Maxmudjon Suyarov (November 5, 2026) and Simon Glenn (November 24, 2026) investigate the monetary and epigraphic apparatus of the Graeco-Bactrians and Indo-Greeks, while Bokiyev (November 19, 2026) and Salayev (November 2, 2026) refine Kushan chronology (127 CE vs. 223/227 CE) via Rabatak, Almosi, and Bactrian tax receipts.

### C. Sirkap/Taxila Under UNESCO Review and Swat Architecture
In Taxila, the *Senate Standing Committee on National Heritage and Culture* inspected Sirkap, Mohra Murado, and the Taxila Museum on November 19, 2026, to review UNESCO concerns regarding structural interventions and to establish strict conservation rules for planned tourism infrastructure. Concurrently, Hilal & Kokab (November 18, 2026) comparatively classify Kushan-era stupa and monastic architecture of the Swat Valley alongside Taxila and Hadda.

### D. Transregional Framework: Ghent, Graz, and Pāli/Vinaya Comparison
The Ghent *Gandhāra Corpora Lecture Series* (November 19 – December 3, 2026) addresses transregional script networks with Ingo Strauch (Indian traders and Gāndhārī-Kharoṣṭhī graffiti in Egypt) and Julian Schott (Arapacana). In comparison, works on the *Mūlasarvāstivāda-Vinaya* (Lei, November 20, 2026) and Pāli *Sutta-Vinaya* methodology (Mudita, November 21, 2026) demonstrate the different paths of monastic oral tradition and early material fixation.

### 1. Executive Summary

During the reporting period of November 2026, peer-reviewed articles, monograph chapters, conference papers, and heritage conservation inspections refine the relationship between administrative literacy, secular chancery practice, and Buddhist book culture in the Taxila–Swat region:

1.  **Gāndhārī Philology & Manuscript Research:** Andrea Schlosser (*Indo-Iranian Journal*, November 4, 2026) analyzes fragment 9 of the Bajaur collection (`BC9r`): On the same birch bark scroll from the 1st–2nd century CE, the recto contains 39 Āryā stanzas on kingship (*rājanīti*, treasury, and foreign policy) in highly Sanskritized Gāndhārī, while the verso bears a Buddhist scholastic text. This materially demonstrates the overlap between secular-administrative and monastic scribal circles.
2.  **Epigraphy & Chancery Practice:** New studies trace the chancery lineage from the Achaemenid *Aramaic Documents from Ancient Bactria* (Marco Ferrario, November 15, 2026) through the genesis of Kharoṣṭhī from Imperial Aramaic (Kadwal & Khakalay, November 14, 2026) and the bilingual coin standards under Menander I (Suyarov, November 5, 2026) to the Bactrian shift under Kaniṣka I (Bokiyev, November 19, 2026).

### 2. Epigraphy & Chancery Practice

The epigraphic contributions of the reporting period strengthen both the model of an Aramaic prehistory (Hypothesis H1) and its continuation under Greek and Central Asian elites (H2).

Marco Ferrario (November 15, 2026, UCL Discovery) investigates the social infrastructure of the Achaemenid eastern satrapies based on the *Aramaic Documents from Ancient Bactria* (ADAB A1) and the Bīsutūn inscription. The Persian imperial administration integrated local power networks through Aramaic-trained chanceries – the milieu attested in Taxila with the Aramaic Aśoka Stele of Sirkap (KAI 273). Shah Mahmoud Kadwal and Ajmal Khakalay (November 14, 2026, *Nangarhar University Journal of Arts and Humanities*) build on this with a script-typological comparison of Imperial Aramaic, Kharoṣṭhī, and Brāhmī, tracing the graphic and phonological transfer from Aramaic chancery practice to Kharoṣṭhī in the Afghanistan–Gandhāra region.

For the Indo-Greek phase, Maxmudjon Suyarov (November 5, 2026) analyzes the transition from Graeco-Bactria to the Indo-Greek state under Menander I based on coin standards (Attic vs. Indian standard) and bilingual Greek-Kharoṣṭhī legends. The continuity into the Kushan period is examined by Anvar Ahmedovich Bokiyev (November 19, 2026, *Oriental Journal of History, Politics and Law*), who combines *Hou Hanshu*, early Kushan coinage, the Rabatak inscription, and the Almosi inscription discovered in Tajikistan in 2022 to form a sequence table from Kujula Kadphises to Kaniṣka I. While new findings on the Swat meridarch Theodotos (CKI 32), the Shāhpur plate, or Macedonian month names were absent in November 2026, the Graz workshop *Epigraphy and New Approaches to Reading (Ancient) Inscriptions* (November 20, 2026) promoted digital imaging techniques for weathered inscriptions.

### 3. Gāndhārī Philology & Manuscript Research

The most important philological individual finding of the month is provided by Andrea Schlosser (November 4, 2026, *Indo-Iranian Journal*) with her preliminary study on fragment 9 of the Bajaur collection (`BC9r`). The birch bark scroll, palaeographically dated to the 1st to 2nd century CE, unites two contrasting text genres on the same support: The recto preserves 39 verses in Āryā meter on *rājanīti* (duties of the king) in highly Sanskritized Gāndhārī in Kharoṣṭhī script, attributed in the colophon to an author named Cakṣuka; the verso, however, bears an independent Buddhist scholastic text in Gāndhārī. `BC9r` discusses the seven-limbed state model (*prakṛti*), royal councils, treasury administration (*kośa*), and foreign policy. Although the text is in the *Arthaśāstra* and *Dharmaśāstra* tradition, it shows no literal dependence on known Sanskrit treatises but reflects an independent Northwest Indian governmental literature with an emphasis on *dharma*. For the question of secular chancery training versus monastic manuscript practice, this material co-presence of statecraft and monastic scholasticism on one and the same scroll is a central finding.

From a linguistic-historical perspective, Mohammad Qaseem Kashaf and Azizullah Jabarkhail (November 22, 2026) classify the transition from Old Indo-Aryan to Middle Indo-Aryan Prakrits through language contact and structural restructuring. Institutionally, Ghent University (November 19 – December 3, 2026) began its hybrid *Gandhāra Corpora Lecture Series*, in which Ingo Strauch (Lausanne) lectures on Indian traders and Gāndhārī-Kharoṣṭhī graffiti in Roman Egypt, and Julian Schott (Vienna) on the Arapacana syllabary. In parallel, the Munich Academy working group *Buddhist Manuscripts from Gandhāra* continues its phase, begun in 2026 (until 2032), towards the completion of the *Dictionary of Gāndhārī* and a historical Kharoṣṭhī palaeography.

### 4. Archaeology Taxila / Sirkap & Gandharan Urbanism

In the archaeology of Taxila and Swat, November 2026 was shaped by heritage conservation, control reports on asset protection, and architectural comparisons. Following the discovery of the large building Jandial D (100 m × 50 m) outside the northern wall of Sirkap, reported in October 2026 by Ca' Foscari and ISMEO, attention in November focused on the preservation of the Taxila World Heritage Site: On November 19, 2026, the *Senate Standing Committee on National Heritage and Culture* inspected the Taxila Museum, Mohra Murado, and the Hippodamian city plan of Sirkap to review UNESCO concerns regarding modern masonry additions. The committee strictly tied future tourism projects (visitor paths, meditation zones) to international conservation standards. Additionally, the *Gandhara Strategic Dialogue 2026* in Islamabad (November 17–20, 2026) aimed at facilitating research and delegation access to sites in Taxila and Swat.

For Swat, Maira Hilal and Rizwan Ullah Kokab (November 18, 2026, *Perspectives in Social Sciences*) present a comparative synthesis on trade, patronage, and Kushan-era sacred architecture. They contrast stupas, monasteries, slate, and stucco decorations in the Swat Valley with findings from Taxila, Peshawar, and Hadda, showing how trade routes shaped an independent Swat workshop tradition alongside the urbanism of Sirkap. No new excavation reports for Barikot were published during the reporting period.

### 5. Historical Cultural Contact & Chronology

Regarding the transition from the Indo-Greeks to the Sakas and Kushans, Simon Glenn (November 24, 2026, Liverpool University Press) provides a new economic-historical evaluation of the Graeco-Bactrian monetary economy as the starting point for Hellenistic chancery structures east of the Hindu Kush in *The Bactrian Mirage*. How trade and script networks changed after the end of Greek rule is investigated by O‘roqov Azamat Turgun ugli (November 20, 2026) in Northern Bactria (Old Termez, Kampyrtepa, Dalverzintepa, Ayrtam), where coin imitations and Bactrian, Greek, and Kharoṣṭhī inscriptions coexisted on the same infrastructure.

Chronologically, Muminjon Salayev (November 2, 2026) examines the starting points of the Bactrian document era (127 CE Kaniṣka era vs. 223/224 or 227/228 CE). Based on internal synchronisms (Sasanian ruler Pērōz, tax receipts), he excludes 127 CE for the later document era and places its beginning in the range of 223/224–227/228 CE, which sharpens the temporal demarcation of the Kushan chancery after the Rabatak inscription.

In comparison to contemporary Buddhist textualization, Xueyang Lei (November 20, 2026, SOAS) investigates the corporate constitution of monasteries in the *Mūlasarvāstivāda-Vinaya*, while Thera Bhikkhu Mudita (November 21, 2026, Zenodo) presents the text-critical methodology of the Pāli *Sutta-Vinaya* tradition. This underscores the contrast between the long orally-mnemonically secured Pāli tradition and the Gāndhārī scrolls materially fixed on birch bark, which transmit both monastic scholasticism and secular statecraft (`BC9r`).
